# From Artificial to Augmentic: How the Meaning of General Intelligence Has Evolved

*Exploring the history of AGI, the changing relationship between humans and intelligent machines, and the possibility of intelligence designed to augment rather than replace us.*

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence has always been more than a technological ambition. It is also an attempt to answer a fundamental question: **What does it mean to be intelligent, and can intelligence exist beyond the human mind?**

For decades, researchers have worked to build machines capable of learning, reasoning, solving problems, and understanding the world. These efforts gave rise to artificial intelligence (AI) and, eventually, the idea of artificial general intelligence (AGI)—a form of intelligence intended to operate across a broad range of tasks rather than remain restricted to a single domain.

However, another possibility deserves attention. What if the ultimate purpose of intelligent machines were not to reproduce human intelligence or surpass it, but to expand what humans can accomplish?

This question introduces the idea of *Augmentic General Intelligence*: a proposed human-centred interpretation of general intelligence in which intelligent systems enhance human reasoning, creativity, learning, and decision-making.

Although this phrase is not an established scientific category, it offers a useful starting point for examining how our understanding of intelligent machines has evolved.

## 1. Before Artificial Intelligence: The Dream of Thinking Machines

The intellectual roots of artificial intelligence extend far beyond modern computers. Philosophers, mathematicians, and inventors have long wondered whether reasoning could be represented through formal rules and mechanical processes.

In 1950, British mathematician Alan Turing published his influential paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*. Rather than attempting to settle the philosophical question of whether machines could think, Turing proposed an imitation game that would later become known as the Turing test.

His work helped turn machine intelligence into a concrete subject of scientific investigation.

At this stage, the central question was whether machines could demonstrate behaviour associated with human intelligence. The emphasis was largely on reproducing intelligent performance, not necessarily on improving human abilities.

This distinction would become increasingly important as computing developed.

## 2. 1956: The Birth of Artificial Intelligence

The expression *artificial intelligence* emerged in the research proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, written in 1955 by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon. The workshop took place in 1956 and is widely regarded as a foundational event in AI research.

The proposal suggested that aspects of learning and intelligence might be described precisely enough for machines to simulate them.

Early researchers investigated symbolic reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, language, and the representation of knowledge. Their ambitions were broad, even though the computers available at the time were extremely limited by modern standards.

The important historical development was the establishment of a research programme around machine intelligence.

Yet the term *artificial intelligence* did not specify whether a machine should imitate a human, outperform a human, or cooperate with one. Those possibilities would develop into different visions of the technology's future.

## 3. From General Intelligence to Narrow AI

As research progressed, the gap between ambitious goals and practical capabilities became increasingly apparent.

Many AI systems became effective at particular tasks: playing games, classifying images, translating text, recommending products, or recognising speech. These systems demonstrated that machines could perform specific forms of intelligent work without possessing a general ability to handle every kind of problem.

This led to a useful distinction:

- **Narrow AI:** Systems designed or trained to perform particular tasks or classes of tasks.
- **Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):** A proposed level of machine intelligence capable of learning and performing across a broad range of domains, with the ability to adapt to unfamiliar tasks.

The boundary is not always perfectly clear. Modern AI systems can perform many different tasks, yet breadth alone does not establish that they possess general intelligence comparable to human intelligence.

The development of narrow AI nevertheless transformed the practical relationship between people and machines. Computers began helping humans analyse information, automate repetitive work, and solve problems at scales that would otherwise be difficult to manage.

Intelligence was no longer only something researchers hoped to reproduce. It had become a capability that people could use.

## 4. The Rise of Artificial General Intelligence

AGI became an important concept for researchers interested in moving beyond systems that excel at individual tasks.

Unlike narrow AI, AGI generally refers to a hypothetical system that can transfer knowledge, learn new skills, reason across different domains, and adapt to unfamiliar situations with substantial flexibility.

However, there is no universally accepted definition or definitive test for AGI. Researchers disagree about the necessary capabilities, the degree of autonomy required, and whether human-level performance across a broad set of tasks is sufficient.

The distinction matters because a system can perform impressively on a wide variety of benchmarks without necessarily possessing the flexible, reliable understanding associated with general intelligence.

AGI therefore represents both a technical research goal and a debate about how intelligence should be measured.

Its development also raises a deeper question: if machines eventually become capable of performing most intellectual tasks, what should their relationship with humanity look like?

One answer focuses on automation. Another focuses on competition. A third places human capability at the centre.

The third perspective provides a bridge to the idea explored in this article.

## 5. From Artificial Intelligence to Intelligence Augmentation

The idea of using computers to enhance human abilities is not new.

In 1960, psychologists and computer scientists J. C. R. Licklider published *Man-Computer Symbiosis*, describing a future in which humans and computers would cooperate closely. In 1962, Douglas Engelbart published *Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework*, proposing that technological systems could improve people's ability to understand complex situations and solve difficult problems.

These ideas established an important alternative to the assumption that computing should simply automate human activity.

Under an augmentation model, a computer does not need to replace a person's reasoning. It can support that reasoning by handling calculations, organising information, revealing patterns, and helping the person explore possibilities.

Consider a scientist investigating a complex biological system. An AI tool might analyse millions of observations, identify possible relationships, and suggest hypotheses. The scientist could then evaluate those hypotheses, design experiments, and interpret the results.

The value of the system would not lie solely in its ability to produce answers. It would also lie in its ability to help a human researcher ask better questions.

This approach shifts the emphasis from *machine intelligence as an independent achievement* to *intelligence as a collaborative capability*.

## 6. What Could “Augmentic General Intelligence” Mean?

The phrase *Augmentic General Intelligence* can be proposed as a name for a human-centred vision of advanced intelligent systems.

It is important to be precise: this is a proposed interpretation, not a recognised replacement for the established term AGI. The following framework is conceptual rather than a claim that a new scientific category has already been defined.

Under this interpretation, Augmentic General Intelligence would describe systems designed to expand human intellectual capabilities across multiple domains while preserving meaningful human agency.

Its defining ambition would be to make people more capable, not merely to make machines more autonomous.

Four principles could help define the concept.

### 6.1 Cognitive augmentation

The system would help people reason through difficult problems, compare explanations, recognise patterns, and identify errors in their thinking.

For example, a student studying physics could use an intelligent assistant to visualise a mathematical relationship, test a hypothesis through simulation, and receive explanations adapted to their understanding.

The objective would be deeper learning rather than simply obtaining the final answer.

### 6.2 General-purpose adaptability

A genuinely augmentic system, as proposed here, would support users across different domains rather than being limited to a single application.

The same system might assist with programming, scientific research, writing, mathematical reasoning, and project planning.

Its usefulness would depend not just on the number of tasks it could perform, but also on its ability to understand context, recognise uncertainty, and adapt its assistance to the user's goals.

### 6.3 Human agency and control

Augmentation should not mean that humans surrender every important decision to an automated system.

People should be able to question recommendations, inspect evidence, set boundaries, and reject machine-generated conclusions.

In high-stakes settings, such as healthcare, scientific research, and public administration, the appropriate level of human oversight would depend on the consequences of error and the reliability of the system.

A system that produces impressive answers but makes users unable to understand or challenge its decisions may increase dependence rather than capability.

### 6.4 Shared intellectual growth

A further ambition would be to make advanced intellectual tools accessible to more people.

If intelligent systems could help students learn difficult subjects, assist researchers with limited resources, and enable small teams to undertake complex projects, they could broaden participation in scientific and technological progress.

However, this outcome would not happen automatically. Access to computing resources, quality education, reliable information, and appropriate infrastructure would remain important.

Augmentation should therefore be evaluated by the capabilities it distributes, not just by the capabilities it demonstrates.

## 7. How AGI and Augmentic General Intelligence Differ

The proposed distinction is primarily one of purpose, not necessarily one of technical capability.

| Dimension | Artificial General Intelligence | Augmentic General Intelligence (proposed) |
|---|---|---|
| Central question | Can a machine perform a broad range of intellectual tasks? | How can general-purpose intelligence expand human capabilities? |
| Primary emphasis | Generality of machine intelligence | Human–machine collaboration |
| Role of humans | May range from users and supervisors to collaborators | Meaningful human participation is a central design goal |
| Measure of success | Broad competence, adaptability, and learning | Improved human capability, understanding, and outcomes |
| Main concern | Building and evaluating general intelligence | Building intelligence that empowers people responsibly |

These categories are not mutually exclusive.

An AGI system could also be designed to augment human intelligence. Conversely, a powerful augmentation system might provide substantial benefits without meeting any accepted definition of AGI.

The distinction is therefore useful as a design philosophy rather than as a claim that two entirely separate technologies must emerge.

## 8. The Challenges of Building Intelligence That Augments Us

A human-centred vision does not eliminate the technical and social difficulties associated with advanced AI.

**Reliability:** An assistant that confidently produces incorrect information can make its user less effective. Systems need ways to communicate uncertainty, support verification, and recover from errors.

**Overdependence:** If people routinely delegate difficult reasoning, they may lose opportunities to develop their own skills. Educational tools should encourage active thinking rather than merely supply solutions.

**Privacy:** Systems that learn from personal work, conversations, or behavioural patterns can create significant privacy risks. Users need appropriate protections and control over their information.

**Inequality:** If advanced cognitive tools are available only to wealthy individuals, companies, or institutions, their benefits may reinforce existing inequalities instead of reducing them.

**Concentration of power:** A small number of organisations controlling the most capable systems could influence how people access information, conduct research, and make decisions.

**Human values:** Intelligence alone does not guarantee wisdom, fairness, or good judgement. Systems need carefully considered objectives, safeguards, and accountability.

These challenges suggest that the success of an augmentic approach cannot be measured exclusively by model performance. It must also be assessed by whether people become better informed, more capable, and more independent.

## 9. A Possible Future: Intelligence as a Collaborative System

Imagine a future in which every student has access to a personalised learning assistant, every independent researcher can use sophisticated analytical tools, and small engineering teams can investigate problems that once required large organisations.

Such a future would not require every person to become an expert in every field. Instead, intelligent systems could lower the barriers to learning and help people apply their existing knowledge more effectively.

A student could use AI to explore a difficult concept, but still work through the underlying mathematics. A programmer could use an assistant to identify potential bugs, but still understand the architecture and evaluate the proposed changes. A scientist could use AI to generate candidate explanations, but still demand evidence before accepting them.

In each example, the machine contributes computational scale and speed, while the human contributes goals, judgement, context, and responsibility.

This is not a guarantee of what future AI will become. It is a direction in which developers, educators, researchers, and policymakers could deliberately guide it.

The central design question would change from “How much work can the machine do without us?” to “How much more can people accomplish with it?”

## Conclusion

The history of artificial intelligence reflects a gradual expansion of ambition: from asking whether machines can exhibit intelligent behaviour, to building systems that perform specialised tasks, to pursuing the possibility of general-purpose machine intelligence.

At the same time, a parallel tradition has explored a different possibility: using computers to augment human intellect.

The proposed idea of **Augmentic General Intelligence** brings these two ambitions together. It imagines general-purpose intelligent systems whose success is measured not only by what they can do independently, but also by how effectively they help people learn, reason, create, and solve problems.

This vision does not require rejecting automation or denying the value of autonomous systems. It requires recognising that technological capability and human benefit are not identical.

A machine may become more capable without making its users more capable. Whether advanced AI produces that outcome will depend on how it is designed, deployed, governed, and incorporated into human life.

Perhaps the most meaningful question about the future of general intelligence is not simply whether machines will become intelligent enough to match us.

It is whether we can build intelligent systems that help humanity become more capable without making people less curious, less independent, or less responsible.
